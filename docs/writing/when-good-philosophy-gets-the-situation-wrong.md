# When good philosophy gets the situation wrong

<p class="article-meta">Published November 17, 2026 · Meldh LLC</p>

An answer can make a reasonable point about responsibility and still ask someone to honor a commitment they never made. The idea may be relevant. The account of what happened is wrong, and that changes what the advice asks the person to do.

This was one of the problems we found while comparing saved answers during Olune's development. Some weaker philosophical responses retained a useful approach to the question but changed a fact, confused a responsibility, or failed to carry a correction into the advice. Reading them as philosophical explanations alone would miss what made them less useful.

Consider a fictional example, invented for this article. Someone agrees to demonstrate a board game at a library's open afternoon. They later discover that the library has advertised a weekly club with them as its regular host. They ask how to correct the announcement without leaving the library in a difficult position.

An imagined answer might emphasize reliability, the value of community activities, and the inconvenience of withdrawing after people have made plans. It might suggest hosting the club for a month before stepping away. Those considerations could matter if the person had agreed to run the club. Here, the answer has treated an agreement to help at one event as a promise to take on a continuing role.

There is still room for a thoughtful response. The person might want to help correct the announcement, preserve the relationship, or offer a limited alternative. They might also need to consider whether anything they said contributed to the misunderstanding. A useful answer would keep the original agreement clear while examining what, if anything, the person should do about the confusion.

## What changed in the answers

A conversation received a full pass when it met every applicable criterion. We read the saved answers and the reasons for their verdicts in cases that gained or lost a full pass between models. That selection tells us about the cases behind a change in score. It leaves open what happened in conversations that kept the same full-pass status.

In one comparison, eleven of the nineteen philosophical conversations that lost a full pass were grouped primarily under problems with facts, participants, or corrections. Some other losses concerned doctrine or terminology. The evidence therefore supports a more specific account than a general claim that the model had lost its philosophical knowledge.

An answer could supply timing that the user had never given, lose a distinction between two responsibilities, or add an unsupported explanation for why something happened. Each change could affect the advice even when the philosophical approach remained recognizable.

The gains mattered too. Other answers improved by withdrawing an allegation after a correction, keeping an uncertain outcome unresolved, or making a proposed duty conditional. Improvements and declines occurred within the same broad capabilities. There was no uniform movement toward either better or worse philosophical reasoning.

## A correction has to reach the advice

Return to the fictional library example. Suppose the person clarifies that they agreed only to the open afternoon. An answer might acknowledge that clarification and then continue recommending a month of hosting to fulfill the supposed promise. The acknowledgment would leave the original error in place.

To use the correction, the answer would have to reconsider why it was recommending that month. Perhaps the person could offer it voluntarily, if they wanted to and had the time. That would be a different reason from owing it as the completion of an agreement. The distinction leaves the person free to weigh an option that the earlier answer presented as a duty.

Fact retention therefore depends on how a detail affects the conclusion. A response can repeat the limit on an agreement and still reason as though the limit does not exist.

A similar problem arises when an answer turns an unknown into an assumption without saying so. Advice about acting before a deadline depends on when the deadline falls. Advice about repairing harm depends on whether the harm has occurred. When that information is missing, a useful response can ask for it or explain how the advice would change under different possibilities. Supplying the missing fact as though the user had stated it makes the recommendation harder to assess.

## What this asks of evaluation

These observations suggest a few practical questions for reviewing philosophical assistance. Has the answer preserved who acted and who agreed to what? Does it distinguish an established fact from an interpretation? When the user corrects something, does the recommendation change where that correction matters?

Those questions do not settle whether the philosophical reasoning itself is sound. An answer can retain the facts and still misuse a concept, make a weak inference, or recommend something unworkable. They help identify a different failure: advice that becomes persuasive only after the answer has changed the situation it is supposed to address.

Our comparisons used familiar cases from our own development work, one sampled set of answers per model, and AI reviewers from the same model family. They were not an independent human benchmark or a study of outcomes for people using the product. The analysis of changed results did not reread stable cases, and a passing conversation could still contain minor defects.

The saved answers help explain the recorded differences in judgments. They do not isolate what caused those differences during model development. Explaining an error in an answer is a different task from establishing which change to a model will prevent it.

When an answer asks someone to act on a principle, we should be able to trace that advice back to facts they supplied, a clearly marked assumption, or a question that still needs answering.
